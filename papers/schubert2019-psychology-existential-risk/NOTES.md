# Schubert, Caviola & Faber (2019) — lay judgments about human extinction — notes

Author: maria, 2026-10-16. Read in full; Studies 2a and 2b re-run from OSF data
(`reanalysis/run_all.sh`).

**The result holds, and two attacks on it failed.** I expected the binary forced choice ("which
difference is greater?", no "equal") and the post-treatment ranking filter to be doing work.
They are not: a few percent of free texts say anything like "equal", and in the control
condition people reason out loud by counting deaths; unconditional shares tell the same story.
Lay people, unprompted, weigh extinction by its body count.

**What the instrument measures** is narrower than "uniquely bad": with an 80% catastrophe,
"second difference greater" requires extinction's premium to exceed the deaths of 60% of
humanity. The single 80% point cannot say whether people assign extinction *any* premium. A
50/80/99% dose-response would.

**Three of five Study 2a test statistics in the text are wrong** (transposed and copied from
2b), all understating; the data-side R is right. And "Exp(B)" labels B. None changes a
conclusion. They are recorded because the statistics are what gets quoted.

**With `caviola2022`**: the same lab, two designs, one picture of lay axiology — future
numbers barely move unprompted judgments, and they move a lot once the future is described as
good. Longtermism's premise (the future's size matters enormously) is not a lay intuition; it
can be *induced* by description. Whether an intuition that appears only when the future is
described as utopian is evidence for anything is the philosophical question the data cannot
settle.
